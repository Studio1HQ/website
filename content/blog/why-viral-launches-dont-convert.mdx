**The short answer:** A viral launch doesn't convert when the views come from people who were never going to use the product, or when interested visitors can't get a result fast. Five things cause it: a hook written for reach, an audience that doesn't fit, a first minute that asks for effort before showing value, a waitlist or capacity limit on the day, and no tracking per channel. Fix those before launch day, then count activated users per channel instead of views.

Most launch guides explain how to get the views. Our guide to [making a launch go viral on X](/blog/viral-launch-on-x) does that. This one covers what happens after: where the visitors go, why most of them leave, and what the launches that turned views into users had in place.

## Can a launch go viral and still get no users?

Yes, and founders who have done it say so. Okara, an AI marketing product, published its [launch handbook](https://okara.ai/viral-launch-x-handbook) in July 2026. Its founder, Fatima Rizwan, writes: "Our most viral launch got 70 million organic views but no product users." The handbook also says that three weeks before Okara's own launch, the team ran a launch that got 70 million views. Then came [the launch of Okara itself](https://x.com/askOkara/status/2033562024651968657) on March 16, 2026. The handbook says that post did 14 million views in a day and brought over 3 million visitors. All of these figures are Okara's own.

![Okara's launch post on X from March 16, 2026: enter your website, try it now, a 50-second demo, 14M views and 54K bookmarks](/blog/uploads/why-viral-launches-dont-convert/okara-launch-post.webp "The launch that converted: what it does in one line, a link to try it that day, and 54K bookmarks.")

The same team ran these launches with the same playbook. The one with a fifth of the views is the one that produced a business. The post above shows why: one line on what the product does, and "Try it now" with a link. The handbook says a visitor types in a website and agents start working before the product asks for anything.

The reverse also happens. Anton Osika [launched Lovable](https://x.com/antonosika/status/1859511355650552018) in November 2024 with a post that has 40.6K views. Four weeks later he [reported $4M in annual recurring revenue](https://x.com/antonosika/status/1870554039462814013). A launch post that few people saw sat in front of a product people paid for.

**Key pointers:**

- Treat views as the cost of a launch, not its result. Two launches by the same team can differ by 5x in views and by everything in users.
- Before you plan reach, write down what a visitor should become: a signup, an install, a lead or a paying customer.
- If a past launch got views and no users, look at the product's first minute before you blame the channel.

## What 2,575 launches show about views and interest

On November 14, 2026 we pulled all 2,575 launch posts from a public directory of product launches on X, dated January 7 to November 13, 2026, and compared how far each post travelled with how people responded to it. The numbers are lifetime totals read from X, and the directory lists launches that got noticed, so the median post in it has 569K views.

X doesn't show signups, so we used the closest public signals of interest: likes and bookmarks per 1,000 views.

| Views on the launch post | Launches | Median likes per 1,000 views | Median bookmarks per 1,000 views |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Under 100K | 616 | 7.0 | 2.7 |
| 100K to 500K | 591 | 3.0 | 1.4 |
| 500K to 1M | 441 | 2.2 | 1.1 |
| 1M to 5M | 712 | 1.5 | 0.8 |
| Over 5M | 215 | 1.3 | 0.5 |

![Chart of median likes per 1,000 views by launch size: 7.0 under 100K views, falling to 1.3 for launches past 5M views, from 2,575 X launches](/blog/uploads/why-viral-launches-dont-convert/views-vs-interest.webp "Likes per view fall by a factor of five between the smallest and largest launches in the set.")

Three things stand out:

1. **Interest per viewer falls as reach grows.** A post that passes 5M views gets about a fifth of the likes per view of one under 100K. That is expected: the first viewers are followers and people in the niche, and every extra million is further from the buyer.
2. **Some big numbers are mostly paid or pushed.** Of the 927 launches past 1M views, 125 (13%) had fewer than 0.3 likes per 1,000 views, a tenth of the median. Reach like that rarely comes from people choosing to share.
3. **What you say changes who responds.** The 208 posts that claimed a first ("world's first", "the first") had a median of 841K views against 549K for the rest, 53% more, and 1.6 likes per 1,000 views against 3.0. The 109 posts that said "open source" had fewer views (342K against 574K) and 5.3 bookmarks per 1,000 views against 1.2. A bookmark is a developer saving something to try later.

Likes and bookmarks are not users. They are the nearest thing X shows, and they move in the opposite direction to views often enough that views alone tell you little.

**Key pointers:**

- Report likes and bookmarks per 1,000 views next to the view count. The median launch in this set got 2.8 likes and 1.2 bookmarks per 1,000 views.
- A launch past 1M views with under 0.3 likes per 1,000 views reached people who didn't care.
- Choose the claim that your buyers save, not the claim that travels furthest.

## Jev: one of 2026's biggest launches, and why it converted

On September 15, 2026, Diogo Almeida [posted the launch of Jev](https://x.com/CompleteSkeptic/status/2099925682726002904), a decision model from his company TypeSafe AI. The post has 40.1M views and 58K bookmarks, from one founder account. The company account's quote of it has 720K.

![Diogo Almeida's Jev launch post on X: a ChatGPT co-inventor hook, three bullets and a 2:52 video, with 40.1M views and 58K bookmarks](/blog/uploads/why-viral-launches-dont-convert/jev-launch-post.webp "Jev's launch post: a credential a stranger can check, three bullets and the founder on camera. 40.1M views.")

Views that size often mean a cold audience. Jev's didn't, and the record of the launch week shows why.

- **People used it and posted what they built.** OpenChamber [studied the launch week on X](https://openchamber.dev/blog/jev-typesafe-ai): 12,759 relevant posts between September 15 and 18. Of those, 3,105 came from 2,172 accounts that had tried Jev. They wrote about routing (604 posts), classification (565), ranking and scoring (263) and tool selection (108).
- **The second wave was other people's posts.** One user's post on pruning context with Jev reached 1.45M views. An explainer reached 400K. A critical post reached 590K. The launch became a topic because developers had something of their own to show.
- **It was usable through tools developers already had.** TypeSafe's own API had a waitlist, but Jev was live on Vercel's AI Gateway and in an OpenRouter beta from the start. A developer could try it that day with a key they already owned.
- **Access was the main complaint, not trust.** OpenChamber counted 507 posts about the waitlist and 363 about having no access, against 112 about trust and 13 about price. TypeSafe [opened access to everyone](https://cryptobriefing.com/typesafe-jev-ai-public-access/) five days after launch.
- **The claims ran ahead of user results.** The homepage said 193.6x faster. Across 215 figures users posted, the median speedup was 7x. Only 4.3% of posts were negative, because 7x was still worth switching for.

The adoption numbers followed. TechCrunch [reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/16/jev-is-vercels-fastest-adopted-ai-model-ever/) that within two weeks of launch, 35% of paid teams on Vercel's AI Gateway had used Jev, nearly double the reach of the second most popular model. Vercel used it too, to classify harmful commands.

Compare that with the 70M-view launch above. Both went viral. Jev had a product people could run on day one, a result they could measure, and a reason to post their own numbers.

**Key pointers:**

- Put the product where your users already have an account: a gateway, a package registry, a marketplace. It removes signup from the first minute.
- Give early users something to measure. Their posts with their own numbers were Jev's second wave.
- Publish a number users will reproduce. A 193x claim against a 7x median invites a study of the gap.

## Five reasons viral launches don't convert

![Diagram of five places a viral launch loses users: the hook, the audience, the first minute, the access and the tracking](/blog/uploads/why-viral-launches-dont-convert/five-leaks.webp "The five leaks between a view and a user. Start with the first minute.")

### 1. The hook was written for reach, not for the buyer

Okara's handbook tells founders to write copy that is "emotional", "divisive" or "a hot take", because replies drive reach. The same handbook describes the cost. After one high-production launch, it says, "there was more discussion on the quality of the video than the real product."

A claim that strangers can check will also be checked. Manus launched as "the first general AI agent", and four days later a [user's teardown](https://x.com/jianxliao/status/1898861051183349870) of its sandbox got more views than the launch post.

**Fix:** write the first line for the person who would pay. Name the job, the stack and the result. Google's [Gemini CLI post](https://x.com/googleaidevs/status/1937861646082515205) said what it was and that it cost nothing, and put the install command in the first reply. Three months later Google said [more than one million developers](https://blog.google/technology/developers/gemini-cli-extensions/) were building with it.

### 2. The wrong people saw it

Reach past your niche is cheap to get and worth little. In the launch data above, 1 in 8 launches past 1M views got almost no likes.

Clerk learned the same thing with YouTube creators. Its team described the program in a session that the Developer Markepear newsletter [summarized in October 2024](https://newslepear.beehiiv.com/p/86-masterclass-into-youtube-influencers-last-sign-up-step-idea-and-more-learnings-on-great-docs). They reviewed more than 10,000 creators and put slightly more of the spend into smaller ones with room to grow than into established names. Long sponsored videos about the product alone "didn't work". Videos where Clerk was one part of a real build did.

**Fix:** pick creators and channels by who their audience is, then rebook on what their signups did. Our guide on [why fit beats reach in developer influencer marketing](/blog/developer-influencer-marketing-strategy) has a scoring method.

### 3. The first minute asks for effort before it shows value

Okara's rule is to "show value in the first 30 seconds, and get to the 'aha' within 60." The handbook lists what breaks it: a survey, a team invite, several onboarding steps. Its test is cheap: "Watch five strangers use it and time them."

For a developer tool, that minute usually ends at the first working result: an install that runs, a first API call that returns 200. We call it time to first hello world, and our [guide to cutting it](/blog/time-to-first-hello-world) lists the blockers in the order developers hit them.

**Fix:** run the stranger test two weeks before launch. Move email verification, card forms and team setup to after the first result.

### 4. There was nothing to use that day

Fundamental Research Labs launched Shortcut, a spreadsheet agent, with a video that asked viewers to comment for an invite code. a16z speedrun [reports](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/how-to-make-a-viral-launch-video) it got 4.1M impressions on X. The follow-up video, when the product was "available now", got another 3.5M. The second post is the one a viewer could act on.

Capacity fails the same way. Within about a week of its July 2025 launch, AWS's Kiro [added daily limits and a waitlist](https://www.techzine.eu/news/devops/133231/usage-restrictions-for-kiro-after-unexpectedly-high-demand/) because demand was higher than expected. And in Jev's launch week, access drew 870 complaints against 112 about trust.

**Fix:** if you need a waitlist, set its end date before launch and plan the "available now" post as a second launch. Load-test signup at 10x your normal peak.

### 5. Nobody could tell which post brought users

One more finding from Clerk's YouTube program: "People didn't click on links in the video description." Clerk judged videos by matching publish dates to signup spikes instead.

In 2025 Clerk tested the same problem with newsletters. Its head of growth marketing paused every newsletter sponsorship for a quarter. Per the [case study TLDR published](https://advertise.tldr.tech/case-studies/clerk-com-uncovers-newsletters-as-a-hidden-engine-behind-12x-traffic-and-30-of-signups/), total traffic fell 20% and signups fell 15% within two months, and newsletters had influenced 12x more traffic than last-click attribution gave them. TLDR sells newsletter ads, so read it as a vendor's account of a customer's test.

**Fix:** give each creator and channel its own link and code, record a 30-day baseline before launch, and ask "where did you hear about us?" on signup. Clicks alone will undercount video and newsletters.

**Key pointers:**

- Check the five in this order: first minute, access, tracking, audience, hook. The first three are in your control weeks before launch.
- A claim of "first" or a multiple users can't reproduce buys views and a teardown.
- Compare channels by what their signups did, not by how many signed up.

## What the launches that converted counted

Few teams publish what happened after the views. These did, and none of them led with the view count.

![Four launches and what came of them: Jev 35% of paid teams, Lovable $4M ARR, Gemini CLI 1M+ developers, Browser Use 5.6x downloads](/blog/uploads/why-viral-launches-dont-convert/past-the-views.webp "Four launches, with the attention number above and the result below.")

| Launch | Attention | What the team counted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jev | 40.1M views on the founder's post | 35% of paid teams on Vercel's AI Gateway within two weeks |
| Lovable | 40.6K views on the launch post | $4M ARR four weeks later |
| Gemini CLI | 3.2M views on the launch post | More than one million developers three months later |
| Browser Use | A 2.4M-view post about Manus using it | Daily downloads [from about 5,000 to 28,000](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/12/browser-use-one-of-the-tools-powering-manus-is-also-going-viral/) in a week |

Jev and Lovable sit a thousand times apart on views and both converted. The attention number didn't predict the result in either direction.

Use the same logic to size your own launch. Start from the result you need and work back. The rates below are placeholders. Replace each one with your own from the last 30 days.

```text
Goal:               200 activated developers
Activation rate:    40% of signups reach a first working result  -> 500 signups
Signup rate:        25% of visitors sign up                      -> 2,000 visits
Visit rate:         0.5% to 1% of views click through            -> 200K to 400K views
```

If your signup rate is 5%, you need five times the views, or a better first minute. The second is usually cheaper.

**Key pointers:**

- Decide before launch which number you'll report in four weeks: revenue, active teams, downloads or meetings.
- One link and one code per creator turns "the launch did 2M views" into a funnel you can fix.
- Size the launch from activated users backwards, and improve the weakest rate before buying more reach.

## The pre-launch conversion check (copy and paste)

Run this two weeks before launch day. A "no" on any of the first four is a reason to move the date.

```text
PRODUCT
[ ] Five people who have never seen the product reached a working result.
    Median time: ____ seconds. Target: under 60 for a hosted product,
    under 5 minutes for an SDK or CLI.
[ ] The first result needs no card, no team invite and no sales call.
[ ] Pricing is on the site and matches what the launch post says.
[ ] Signup and the first run were load-tested at 10x a normal peak.

ACCESS
[ ] A visitor can use the product on launch day.
[ ] If there is a waitlist: its end date is set, and the "available now"
    post is drafted.

MESSAGE
[ ] The first line names who it is for and what they get.
[ ] Someone outside the team reproduced every number in the post.
[ ] The post makes sense without the link and the video.

TRACKING
[ ] One UTM link and one code per creator and per channel.
[ ] "Where did you hear about us?" is on the signup form.
[ ] A 30-day baseline is recorded: visits, signups, activation rate.
[ ] Activation is defined as one product event, and it is logged.

AFTER
[ ] Day 3: activated users per channel, not views, in the retro.
[ ] Day 14: retention by channel, then decide who to rebook.
[ ] The next launch moment has a date.
```

Our launch guide has a [worksheet of metrics by stage](/blog/how-to-launch-a-developer-tool) and a definition of activation for each kind of developer tool.

**Key pointers:**

- The product and access rows decide conversion more than anything on launch day.
- Define activation as a single logged event before the launch, or you can't compare channels after it.
- Hold the retro on day 3 for activation and again on day 14 for retention.

## One launch is not the plan

Nico Christie of Fundamental Research Labs told a16z speedrun: "A launch is a weapon, not the goal." The data agrees that the first launch is often not the biggest. In the launch directory, 338 of 1,690 companies appear with two or more launches, and for 154 of them (46%) the most-viewed launch was not their first.

Lovable shows what the later posts carry. Its launch post has 40.6K views. The post a month later, ["$4m ARR in 4 weeks, this is how we did it"](https://x.com/antonosika/status/1870554039462814013), has 1.2M. A result is a stronger second launch than a second announcement.

Tibo, an indie founder, did the same after [launching Squad](https://x.com/tibo_maker/status/2095449430619009085) on September 3, 2026. Four days later he [posted](https://x.com/tibo_maker/status/2096893802367565831) a revenue chart, and on September 18 he posted that Squad [had reached $10k MRR](https://x.com/tibo_maker/status/2100980539184251207).

![Tibo's post on X four days after launching Squad: revenue has spiked to $8k MRR, with a revenue chart, 93.6K views](/blog/uploads/why-viral-launches-dont-convert/squad-revenue-post.webp "Four days after launch, a post with 93.6K views reports the number that counts: revenue.")

A launch that got views and few users still gave you something: a log of what strangers asked, where they dropped and what they expected the product to be. Okara's team used launches that way, as a loop to "build a product, do a launch, get feedback, decide on the fate of the product."

**Key pointers:**

- Plan the results post when you plan the launch: a revenue, usage or customer number you'll be able to share in two to four weeks.
- After a launch that didn't convert, fix the leak the data points to and relaunch on the next feature.
- Keep a log of launch-day questions. It tells you what the first minute failed to explain.

## FAQ

### Why did my launch go viral but get no signups?

Usually for one of five reasons: the post was written to spread and not to tell buyers what the product does, the people who saw it don't have the problem, the product needs more than a minute to show value, there was a waitlist or an outage on the day, or the signups happened and you couldn't trace them. Check the first minute and the tracking first.

### What is a good conversion rate from launch views to signups?

There is no standard, and few launches publish the full funnel. Lovable's launch post had 40.6K views and the product reached $4M ARR in four weeks. Other launches pass 1M views with almost no response. Use your own visit, signup and activation rates from the last 30 days and work back from the number of activated users you need.

### Do views on a launch post matter at all?

They matter as the top of the funnel and for the second-order effects: investor interest, hiring and brand search. They don't predict users. In 2,575 X launches, likes per 1,000 views fell from 7.0 for posts under 100K views to 1.3 for posts past 5M, and 13% of launches past 1M views had almost no likes at all.

### Why did Jev's launch convert when other viral launches didn't?

Developers could use it on day one through Vercel's AI Gateway and OpenRouter, and it gave them a result they could measure. In four days, 2,172 accounts posted about trying it, and within two weeks 35% of paid teams on Vercel's AI Gateway had used it. The main complaint was the waitlist on TypeSafe's own API, which ended after five days.

### Should I launch with a waitlist?

Only if you can't serve the traffic, and then with an end date. A waitlist turns the day of highest attention into a day when nobody can use the product. Fundamental's "available now" follow-up got 3.5M impressions after a 4.1M invite-only launch, so plan the open-access post as a launch of its own.

### How do I track which launch post or creator brought users?

Give each creator and channel its own UTM link and signup code, add a "where did you hear about us?" question to signup, and record a 30-day baseline before launch. Clerk found viewers didn't click links in video descriptions, so compare publish dates with signup spikes as well as counting clicks.

### What should I measure after a launch instead of views?

Activated users per channel: people who reached a first working result, such as an install that ran or a first successful API call. Then check retention by channel at day 14. On the post itself, track likes and bookmarks per 1,000 views to see whether the reach came from people who cared.

### What should I do after a launch that didn't convert?

Read the replies and the signup drop-off to find which of the five leaks it was, fix that one, and launch again on the next feature or result. Among companies with more than one launch in the data we studied, 46% got their most-viewed launch on a later attempt.

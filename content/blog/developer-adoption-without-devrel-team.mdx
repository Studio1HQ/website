**The short answer:** You can get developer adoption without a DevRel team by splitting the DevRel job across the people you already have. Make the first run work in minutes, publish content that answers the questions developers ask when they evaluate your category, post it in the communities where those questions come up, and turn every recurring question into a docs fix or a release. The founder usually owns distribution and one engineer owns the quickstart and docs. When the plan is clear but nobody has the hours, outsourced or fractional DevRel, such as Studio1, can run the execution while your team keeps building the product.

Fix the first run before anything else.")

## What a DevRel hire would own

At an early-stage devtool, a developer relations hire usually owns five jobs:

1. **The first run.** Getting a new developer from the docs to a working result.
2. **Content.** Tutorials, guides and comparisons that answer evaluation questions.
3. **Distribution.** Posting that content where developers already talk.
4. **Demos and examples.** Sample apps and repos people can clone and run.
5. **Community and feedback.** Answering questions and carrying what developers say back to the product team.

None of these needs a DevRel title. Each one needs a named owner and a weekly slot on the calendar. The rest of this guide splits them across a founder and an engineering team, then covers when outside help makes sense.

A full-time hire is also slow and expensive. Our [DevRel agency vs in-house hire comparison](/blog/devrel-agency-vs-in-house-devrel-hire) works through an illustrative US scenario of about $195,000 a year in salary and benefits, and about 9 weeks to hire before notice periods and ramp-up. If you are still finding product-market fit, that is often the wrong first move.

## Step 1: Fix the first run before you ask for attention

Distribution sends developers to your docs. If the quickstart breaks, every post you publish makes the problem bigger.

* **Run the quickstart like a stranger.** Use a fresh machine or container, no internal knowledge, and a timer. Write down every step where you had to guess.
* **Get to a working result in minutes.** One install command, one example, one output the developer can see.
* **Pick the path for them.** If your product has two ways in, such as an SDK and a hosted gateway, say on the first page which one to choose.
* **Make errors say what to do next.** A clear error message saves a support thread.

**Owner:** one engineer, with a recurring slot every week.

For [Corsair](/case-studies/corsair), Studio1 started with an onboarding audit of the hosted app, the docs and the first-run flow. The Corsair team used it to rework the quickstart so it reaches a working example in under a minute. For [LiteLLM](/case-studies/litellm), we worked as the embedded docs and DX team: a new Learn section as the starting point, separate SDK and Gateway quickstarts that take developers to a first working request in minutes, and a clear split between the two paths. LiteLLM's CEO endorsed the revamp publicly.

If you want an outside read of your first run, that is what a [developer documentation and DX audit](/developer-documentation-dx-audit) covers.

## Step 2: Publish content that answers evaluation questions

Developers evaluate tools by searching, asking AI assistants, and reading threads. Your content should be the answer they find.

* **List the questions.** Write down the 5 to 10 questions a developer asks before picking a tool in your category: "how do I do X with Y", "A vs B", "is there an open-source alternative to Z".
* **Write one piece per question.** A tutorial, an integration guide or a comparison, each with code that runs.
* **Start from a real tension.** Open with a problem developers already argue about, not with your feature list.
* **Have engineers write or review.** Developers check the code before they trust the claim.

**Owner:** the founder picks the questions; engineers write or review the drafts.

For [Entelligence AI](/case-studies/entelligence-ai), the posts that worked opened with a developer tension, such as code reviewers seeing more context than ever while production keeps breaking, instead of abstract AI positioning.

## Step 3: Distribute where developers already talk

Publishing on your own blog is not distribution. Each piece needs a version for the places your users read.

* **Reddit.** Lead with the problem, post in the subreddits where it is discussed, and reply to every serious comment.
* **Hacker News.** Use Show HN only for something people can try, and stay in the thread to answer questions.
* **X and LinkedIn.** A short thread or article that explains the idea in plain terms, with a demo clip.
* **Dev.to and daily.dev.** Cross-post with a canonical link back to your site.

Adapt the post for each channel instead of pasting the same text everywhere. Entelligence AI's blog angles, rewritten as standalone Reddit discussions, generated 304K+ Reddit impressions. [Memori's](/case-studies/memori) Reddit launch post reached 85+ upvotes and 50K+ impressions. Our [guide to distributing technical content on Reddit, X and YouTube](/blog/how-to-distribute-technical-content-reddit-x-youtube) covers each channel in detail.

**Owner:** the founder, who can answer technical questions in a thread without checking with anyone.

## Step 4: Ship demos and sample repos

Developers trust what they can run. A demo turns "interesting" into "let me try it."

* Build several small projects that solve recognizable problems, not one generic hello world.
* Make each one run locally with minimal setup, in a public repo.
* Pair each demo with a short post or video, and link it from your README and docs.

**Owner:** engineers. Treat demos like features: scoped, reviewed and shipped.

Studio1 built 12 hands-on demo agents for Memori, including a Wellness Coach, an arXiv Researcher and a Customer Support Voice Agent, and amplified them through the [Awesome AI Apps](https://github.com/Arindam200/awesome-ai-apps) repository. For Corsair, demos like Brieflane (meeting transcripts to Linear issues and Slack updates) and SlideFlow (a prompt to slides, published to Drive and Slack) gave developers something to run instead of a list of integrations.

## Step 5: Run community and feedback loops

Adoption compounds when developers see their questions turn into fixes.

* **Pick one home for questions.** GitHub Discussions, Discord or Slack. Answer within a day.
* **Rotate who answers.** A rotation across engineers holds up better than one overloaded person.
* **Route every repeat question.** If two developers ask the same thing, it becomes a docs fix, an issue or a roadmap item.
* **Show up in person.** Meetups, talks and hackathons give you feedback a social post cannot.

**Owner:** the whole engineering team, on rotation.

For Memori, a thought-leadership Reddit post drew 100+ detailed developer comments that shaped onboarding, docs and the Memori v2 roadmap, and a MongoDB Kolkata meetup talk drew 150+ attendees. For LiteLLM, Studio1 answered developer questions in the community and fed recurring patterns back into the docs, which reduced future support load. For [OpenLore](/case-studies/openlore), GitHub stars jumped after each talk and hackathon session.

## Step 6: Know when to bring in outside help

Covering DevRel with your own team works while the product is small and the founder has time. It stops working when distribution and demos slip every week because the roadmap wins.

| Situation | Best fit |
| --- | --- |
| You need direction, not more hands | A [fractional DevRel](/blog/fractional-devrel) lead for a few hours a week |
| The plan is clear, but nobody has the hours | An agency that runs content, demos, distribution and events with your team |
| A launch is coming and you need one strong week | A launch-scoped engagement, as in our [Product Hunt launch playbook](/blog/product-hunt-launch-playbook) |
| Community is the product and needs daily ownership | A full-time DevRel hire |

For OpenLore, a one-month engagement covering a DX audit, launch positioning, talks and daily distribution took the repo from about 135 to 1,700+ GitHub stars and 100K+ impressions. Many teams end up with a hybrid: an internal owner keeps the relationship with the community, and outside help adds production capacity.

## What to measure

Reach is the easiest number to collect and the least useful on its own. Track the path from reach to use:

1. **Reach:** impressions and visits from each channel.
2. **First run:** how many visitors complete the quickstart, and how long it takes them.
3. **Activation:** first API call, first deploy or first project created.
4. **Retention:** developers still active after 30 days.
5. **Community signals:** questions asked, issues opened, contributors, and GitHub stars for open-source projects.

![Five adoption metrics in order: reach, first run, activation, retention and community signals, with activation marked as where reach becomes use](/blog/uploads/developer-adoption-without-devrel-team/adoption-metrics-path.webp "Track each step from reach to use, not reach alone.")

Record a baseline before you start, so you can tell what moved the numbers. Measurable adoption results often take 3 to 6 months, so set early milestones for the first run and content output alongside the longer-term goals.

## FAQ

### Can you get developer adoption without a DevRel team?

Yes. Early-stage teams can cover the DevRel job by splitting it: one engineer owns the quickstart and docs, the founder owns distribution, engineers ship demos, and the whole team routes community questions into fixes. What matters is a named owner and a weekly slot for each job, not the title.

### What should a founder do first to drive developer adoption?

Run your own quickstart like a stranger and fix every step that breaks. Then list the questions developers ask when they evaluate your category and publish one useful answer per question, with code that runs. Distribution only pays off once the first run works.

### Is outsourced DevRel worth it for an early-stage startup?

It is worth it when you know what needs to happen but nobody has the hours, or when a launch is coming. Look for a partner whose engineers write the content and build the demos, and who reports on activation as well as reach. If you only need direction, a fractional DevRel lead costs less. Our [agency vs in-house comparison](/blog/devrel-agency-vs-in-house-devrel-hire) breaks down the costs.

### When should you hire a full-time DevRel?

Hire when community and developer education are core to the product and need daily ownership, when you already have a working first run and a content process for the hire to scale, and when you can wait the months it takes to hire and ramp. If you hire before the basics work, your first DevRel will likely spend their first quarter fixing docs.

### How long does it take to see developer adoption results?

First-run fixes reach every new developer from the day they ship. Content and community take longer, and measurable adoption results often take 3 to 6 months. Focused launch work can move faster: OpenLore grew its GitHub stars 12x in one month.
